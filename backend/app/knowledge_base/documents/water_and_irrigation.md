# Water, Irrigation and Drainage

Source: ICAR water management guidance; TNAU Agritech Portal. Compiled for Agri
AI's farming assistant.

Water is the input farmers most often get wrong in both directions — too little
and the crop is stunted, too much and the roots suffocate and disease takes hold.
Most foliar disease in vegetables is also a water problem, because wet leaves are
what fungal and bacterial pathogens need.

## Why water management is disease management

- **Splashing water carries inoculum.** Soil-borne pathogens such as *Alternaria*,
  *Septoria* and *Xanthomonas* reach the leaves on water splashed up from the
  soil surface. Base-only irrigation removes this route entirely.
- **Leaf wetness is the trigger.** Most fungal spores need a period of continuous
  leaf wetness to infect. A canopy that dries quickly between waterings is far
  harder to infect.
- **Waterlogging causes root disease.** Saturated soil has no oxygen, roots die
  back, and *Pythium*, *Phytophthora* and bacterial wilt move in. A plant with
  damaged roots also shows yellowing that is easily mistaken for nutrient
  deficiency or leaf disease.

## The right way to irrigate

**Water at the base, not over the canopy.** Drip or furrow irrigation keeps the
foliage dry. If you must use a sprinkler, water in the early morning so the leaves
dry during the day — never in the evening, which leaves them wet all night.

**Water deeply and less often.** Shallow, frequent watering encourages a shallow
root system that cannot withstand a dry spell. A deeper soak encourages roots
downward.

**Match the schedule to the crop stage.** Young plants need little; flowering and
fruit or tuber development need the most; and near maturity the requirement drops
again. Water stress during flowering causes flower drop and poor fruit set.

**Check before you water.** Push a finger or a small trowel into the soil. If it
is moist 5 cm down, you do not need to water yet, whatever the calendar says.

## Drainage matters as much as irrigation

- Make sure the field has a way for excess water to leave — a slight slope, or
  open drains at the low end.
- In heavy soils, form raised beds so roots sit above the waterlogged zone.
- After heavy rain, walk the field and drain any standing water. Even 24 hours of
  waterlogging damages most vegetable roots.
- Do not let water from an affected patch drain into a healthy one.

## Mulching

A mulch of straw, crop residue or plastic film:

- reduces evaporation, so less water is needed;
- moderates soil temperature;
- suppresses weeds;
- and, most importantly for disease, blocks the soil splash that carries
  pathogens onto the lower leaves.

This is one of the cheapest and most effective disease-prevention measures
available, and it is usually overlooked.

## Irrigation method by situation

| Situation | Recommended | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Vegetables, high-value crops | Drip | Precise, keeps foliage dry, allows fertigation |
| Field crops, level land | Furrow | Simple, keeps water off the canopy |
| Water-scarce areas | Drip with mulch | Largest saving per rupee |
| Sloping land | Furrow with contour | Reduces runoff and erosion |
| Nursery | Fine rose can or drip | Seedlings are easily damaged by a strong stream |

## Water quality

- **Salinity.** Repeated use of saline water builds salts in the root zone and
  reduces yield. Leach them out with a heavier irrigation occasionally.
- **Hard water and alkaline water** can raise soil pH over time, which affects
  nutrient availability.
- **Never irrigate with water that has drained from a field with bacterial wilt**
  or any root disease.

## Practical summary

1. Water at the base. Keep the leaves dry.
2. Water in the morning if you must water overhead.
3. Water deeply and less often, not shallowly and often.
4. Mulch — for moisture and for disease.
5. Make sure water can leave the field as easily as it arrives.
6. Check the soil before watering rather than following a fixed calendar.
