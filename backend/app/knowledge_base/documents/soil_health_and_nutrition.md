# Soil Health, Nutrition and Composting

Source: ICAR soil health guidance; TNAU Agritech Portal. Compiled for Agri AI's
farming assistant.

Most disease problems a farmer sees on the leaf began in the soil. A crop that is
well fed and growing in living soil resists infection far better than a stressed
one. Nutrition is not separate from plant protection — it is the first line of it.

## Start with a soil test

A soil test tells you what your soil actually has, rather than what a standard
bag of fertiliser assumes it has. Applying a fixed dose to every field wastes
money on some and starves others. Test every two to three years, and take the
sample properly:

- Sample from 8-10 spots across the field in a zig-zag pattern.
- Take soil from 15-20 cm depth for field crops.
- Mix the samples in a clean bucket, quarter it, and send about 500 g.
- Avoid sampling near a bund, a manure heap, or where a crop has just been
  harvested.

Soil health cards are issued through state agriculture departments and KVKs, and
usually carry a crop-specific fertiliser recommendation on the back.

## The major nutrients, in plain terms

**Nitrogen (N)** drives leaf and stem growth. Too little gives pale, stunted
plants. Too much gives dark, soft, leafy growth that is *more* attractive to
pests and more prone to fungal disease — over-application is a real risk, not a
safety margin.

**Phosphorus (P)** drives root development and early establishment. It matters
most at sowing and transplanting.

**Potassium (K)** drives fruit and tuber development, water regulation and stem
strength. Potassium-deficient plants show early blight and similar diseases
sooner and more severely. This is one of the most commonly deficient nutrients in
intensive vegetable systems.

**Secondary and micronutrients** — calcium, magnesium, sulphur, zinc, boron, iron
— matter in small quantities but cause real problems when absent. Boron
deficiency, for example, causes hollow stem and poor fruit set, and is often
mistaken for disease.

## Organic matter: the part that is usually neglected

Organic matter is what makes soil hold water and nutrients, and what feeds the
organisms that suppress disease. Indian soils under continuous chemical farming
commonly fall below 0.5% organic carbon, which is low.

Ways to build it:

- **Farmyard manure** — well-rotted, applied before sowing. Never use fresh
  manure; it burns roots and carries weed seed and pathogens.
- **Compost** — crop residue, cattle dung and kitchen waste, turned and kept
  moist until dark, crumbly and odourless.
- **Vermicompost** — earthworm-processed material, richer in available nutrients
  and microbial life than ordinary compost.
- **Green manure** — grow sunhemp, dhaincha or a legume and plough it in before
  flowering.
- **Crop residue** — retain what you can rather than burning it. Burning destroys
  the organic matter and the soil life with it.
- **Mulching** — a straw or plastic mulch conserves moisture, moderates soil
  temperature, and blocks the soil splash that spreads foliar disease.

## Composting that actually works

A compost pile needs four things: the right mix of materials, moisture, air, and
time.

- **Mix browns and greens.** Dry material (straw, dry leaves) provides carbon;
  green material (cattle dung, fresh residue, kitchen waste) provides nitrogen.
  Roughly 3 parts brown to 1 part green by volume.
- **Keep it as moist as a wrung-out cloth.** Too dry and nothing happens; too wet
  and it goes anaerobic and smells.
- **Turn it every 2-3 weeks** to bring air in.
- **It is ready when** it is dark, crumbly, has an earthy smell, and you cannot
  recognise the original material — typically 2-3 months.

**Do not compost diseased plant material** for use on the same crop. Most home
compost piles do not reach the temperature needed to kill pathogens.

## Biofertilisers and soil biological health

- **Rhizobium** — for legumes; fixes atmospheric nitrogen in root nodules.
- **Azotobacter / Azospirillum** — free-living nitrogen fixers for cereals and
  vegetables.
- **Phosphate solubilising bacteria (PSB)** — release phosphorus already in the
  soil but unavailable to the plant.
- ***Trichoderma* and *Pseudomonas*** — as much soil health agents as disease
  control, since they colonise roots and compete with pathogens.
- **Mycorrhizal fungi (VAM)** — extend the root system's reach for water and
  phosphorus.

Treat biofertilisers as living products: store them cool, out of direct sun, and
never mix them with chemical fungicides or with concentrated fertiliser solutions.

## Practical order of operations

1. Get a soil test. Do not guess.
2. Build organic matter every season — it is slow but compounding.
3. Apply the major nutrients to the soil test recommendation.
4. Correct micronutrient deficiencies, which are usually small and cheap to fix.
5. Use biofertilisers to make what is already in the soil available.
6. Monitor the crop and adjust, rather than following a fixed calendar.

## A note on confidence

Nutrition problems and disease problems look alike on a leaf. Yellowing can be
nitrogen deficiency, waterlogging, root damage, or a virus. Before treating for
disease, rule out nutrition and water — it is cheaper and often the real answer.
