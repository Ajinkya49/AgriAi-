# Tomato Diseases — Identification and Management

Source: ICAR crop protection advisories; TNAU Agritech Portal; published plant
pathology literature. Compiled for Agri AI's farming assistant.

Tomato (*Solanum lycopersicum*) is attacked by several foliar diseases that look
similar to an untrained eye but need different responses. Getting the
identification right matters more than the spray schedule.

## Early Blight (*Alternaria solani*)

**What it looks like.** Dark brown spots with concentric rings, like a target, on
the older and lower leaves first. The tissue around each spot turns yellow. In
heavy infections the leaves dry up and drop, and the fruit can develop dark
sunken lesions at the stem end.

**When it strikes.** Warm, humid weather with alternating wet and dry periods.
It usually appears after flowering and is worse in plants under stress — low
nitrogen, poor nutrition, or heavy fruit load.

**Why it starts low.** The fungus survives in the soil and in infected crop
debris. Rain or overhead irrigation splashes spores onto the lowest leaves, which
is why the disease almost always begins there.

**Management.**
- Remove and destroy affected lower leaves as soon as the target spots appear.
  Bag them and take them out of the field; do not compost them.
- Mulch around the base and water at the root, not over the canopy, to break the
  soil-to-leaf route.
- Stake plants and remove lower leaves so the canopy dries quickly.
- Rotate away from tomato, potato and other solanaceous crops for at least two
  seasons.
- Spray *Pseudomonas fluorescens* at 5 g per litre of water in the evening,
  covering both leaf surfaces. Biocontrol works best preventively and repeatedly.
- Apply *Trichoderma viride* to the soil at 5 g per litre as a drench, or mix
  5 g per kg into the soil at transplanting.

## Late Blight (*Phytophthora infestans*)

**What it looks like.** Large, irregular water-soaked grey-green patches that
turn brown and papery. In humid weather a pale fuzzy white mould appears on the
underside of the leaf. The whole plant can collapse within days.

**When it strikes.** Cool, wet weather — prolonged leaf wetness is the trigger.
This is the most destructive tomato disease and it moves extremely fast.

**Management.**
- **Uproot and destroy infected plants immediately.** Do not wait to see how it
  develops. This is the single most important action.
- Stop overhead irrigation and open up the canopy so foliage dries.
- Apply Bordeaux mixture as a *protectant* — it must be on the leaf before
  infection, and needs re-applying after rain.
- Plant resistant varieties. Ask your KVK or state agriculture department which
  varieties recommended for your district carry late blight tolerance.

**Do not confuse late and early blight.** Early blight has target-like rings and
starts on old leaves; late blight has irregular water-soaked patches and white
mould underneath. Late blight demands immediate action; early blight is managed
over the season.

## Leaf Mold (*Passalora fulva*)

**What it looks like.** Pale yellow spots on the upper leaf surface that turn
brown, with a velvety olive-green to brown mould developing on the underside.

**When it strikes.** High humidity and poor air movement — it is mainly a
protected-cultivation and polyhouse problem, and rarely severe in open fields.

**Management.** This is a humidity disease, so ventilation is the cure. Prune
lower leaves, widen spacing, open vents or side curtains, and water at the base
only. Remove affected leaves and clear debris at the end of the season.

## Septoria Leaf Spot (*Septoria lycopersici*)

**What it looks like.** Many small circular spots with grey centres and dark
margins, starting on the lowest leaves. Tiny black dots (the spore bodies) are
visible inside the spots. Leaves yellow and drop prematurely.

**When it strikes.** Warm, wet weather. Like early blight it starts low and
climbs, and it survives in soil and debris between seasons.

**Management.** Strip infected lower leaves early, mulch to block soil splash,
rotate for two seasons, and keep the canopy dry.

## General prevention that applies to all of them

1. **Start clean.** Certified seed, clean nursery, and a bed free of last
   season's debris.
2. **Keep the foliage dry.** Drip or furrow irrigation at the base rather than
   overhead sprinklers. Water in the morning if you must water overhead.
3. **Open the canopy.** Staking, pruning lower leaves and correct spacing all
   reduce the leaf wetness that these diseases need.
4. **Feed the plant properly.** Stressed plants get sick sooner. Follow a soil
   test rather than a fixed fertiliser dose.
5. **Rotate.** Two seasons away from tomato, potato and other solanaceous crops
   reduces soil-borne inoculum substantially.
6. **Scout twice a week.** Check the oldest, lowest leaves first — that is where
   most of these diseases appear, and catching them there is far easier.

## A note on confidence

A photograph can suggest a disease but cannot confirm it. Symptoms overlap, and
nutrient deficiency, herbicide drift and mite damage can all mimic disease
patterns. Where a diagnosis matters, confirm with your local KVK or extension
officer before spending money on treatment.
