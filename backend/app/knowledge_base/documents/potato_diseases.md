# Potato Diseases — Identification and Management

Source: ICAR crop protection advisories; TNAU Agritech Portal; published plant
pathology literature. Compiled for Agri AI's farming assistant.

Potato (*Solanum tuberosum*) is the most important tuber crop in India and is
attacked by two foliar blights that look similar but behave very differently.
Late blight can destroy a field in days; early blight is a slower, seasonal
problem. Distinguishing them is the first decision a farmer has to make.

## Late Blight (*Phytophthora infestans*)

**Why it matters.** Late blight is the most destructive potato disease in India.
Under cool, wet conditions it can take a field from healthy to collapsed within a
week, and it destroys tubers in store as well as in the field.

**What it looks like.** Water-soaked grey-green lesions that enlarge rapidly into
brown necrotic patches. In damp weather a white mould appears on the underside of
the leaf, and the leaf margins curl. The plant has a distinct musty smell in
advanced stages. Tubers develop a shallow brown rot.

**When it strikes.** Cool (18-22°C) and wet weather with prolonged leaf wetness.
Overcast days with drizzle are the classic trigger. Once established, it spreads
on wind and rain splash.

**Management.**
- **Destroy infected plants at once.** Uproot and remove them from the field
  entirely. Do not wait, and do not leave the material at the field edge.
- **Earth up the ridges properly.** Well-formed ridges reduce the chance of
  spores washing down onto the tubers, which is what turns a foliar outbreak into
  a storage loss.
- **Water at the base only.** Overhead irrigation extends leaf wetness and drives
  the epidemic.
- **Apply Bordeaux mixture as a protectant.** It must be on the leaf before
  infection; re-apply after rain.
- **Plant a resistant variety.** Ask your KVK or state agriculture department
  which varieties recommended for your district carry late blight resistance.
- **Destroy the haulm before harvest** if blight is present, and wait before
  lifting so spores on the soil surface die off.

## Early Blight (*Alternaria solani*)

**What it looks like.** Brown to black spots with concentric rings on the older
leaves, often with a yellow halo. Lesions merge as the disease progresses and the
leaf dries out and drops. It moves upward through the canopy over the season.

**When it strikes.** Warm, humid weather, and it is worse in plants under stress
— particularly low potassium and poor nutrition. It usually appears after tuber
initiation.

**Management.**
- Remove affected lower foliage and destroy crop debris at the end of the season.
- Follow a soil test and maintain balanced nutrition, especially potassium.
  Stressed plants show early blight sooner and more severely.
- Apply *Trichoderma viride* to the soil at planting or as a drench at 5 g per
  litre of water.
- Spray neem-based preparations every 7-10 days while the weather stays humid.

## Common Scab (*Streptomyces scabies*)

**What it looks like.** Rough, corky, brown lesions on the tuber skin. It does
not usually reduce yield much but it badly affects market value.

**Management.** The organism prefers dry, alkaline soil. Keep the soil moist
during tuber initiation, avoid fresh manure and lime before planting, and rotate
away from potato and beet for at least three years.

## Bacterial Wilt (*Ralstonia solanacearum*)

**What it looks like.** Sudden wilting of the whole plant while the leaves are
still green, followed by yellowing and collapse. Cutting the stem reveals brown
discolouration inside and a milky bacterial ooze.

**Management.** There is no cure once a plant is infected. Remove and destroy
affected plants, do not save seed tubers from an affected field, rotate away from
potato, tomato and brinjal for at least three seasons, and improve drainage.

## General prevention that applies to all of them

1. **Use certified, disease-free seed tubers.** This is the single most effective
   control for the seed- and soil-borne problems.
2. **Rotate** away from potato, tomato and brinjal. A cereal or legume in between
   reduces soil-borne inoculum.
3. **Keep foliage dry.** Base-only irrigation and morning watering.
4. **Maintain ridges** so spores and water do not reach the tubers.
5. **Feed the crop properly.** A soil test is cheaper than a lost crop.
6. **Scout after every wet spell.** Late blight can appear within days of cool,
   wet weather — walk the field and check the lower leaves and undersides first.

## Storing the harvest

Late blight and soft rot both develop in store. Only store sound, dry tubers.
Cure them in a dark, ventilated place for 10-15 days before moving them into
longer-term storage, and discard any tuber that is already showing rot. Never
store blighted tubers with healthy ones.

## A note on confidence

A photograph can suggest a disease but cannot confirm it. Symptoms overlap with
nutrient deficiency, herbicide injury and mite damage. Where the diagnosis
matters, confirm with your local KVK or extension officer before spending money
on treatment.
