# Pepper (Chilli and Bell Pepper) Diseases — Identification and Management

Source: ICAR crop protection advisories; published plant pathology literature.
Compiled for Agri AI's farming assistant.

Pepper (*Capsicum* spp.) — chilli and bell pepper — is grown across India as both
a rainfed and an irrigated crop. Its most common bacterial disease is seed-borne,
which is why seed handling matters more here than in most crops.

## Bacterial Spot (*Xanthomonas* spp.)

**What it looks like.** Small water-soaked spots on the leaves that turn brown to
black and become angular, because the veins limit their spread. Severely affected
leaves yellow and drop, leaving the fruit exposed to sunscald. On the fruit,
spots are raised, dark and scabby.

**Why it starts with the seed.** The bacteria survive inside and on the seed coat.
Sowing infected seed introduces the disease into a clean field, and no amount of
spraying afterwards will undo that. This is the key fact about bacterial spot.

**Management.**
- **Start with certified disease-free seed.** If you save your own seed, treat it
  in hot water at 50°C for 25 minutes, then cool it immediately in cold water and
  dry it in the shade before sowing. Do not guess at the temperature — too hot
  kills the seed, too cool does not kill the bacteria.
- **Remove infected plants and clear debris** at the end of the season. The
  bacteria survive in crop residue and on volunteer plants.
- **Avoid overhead irrigation**, and do not prune or harvest while the foliage is
  wet — the bacteria spread on splashing water and on hands and tools.
- **Rotate** away from pepper and tomato for at least one season.
- **Copper-based sprays** such as Bordeaux mixture are used as a protectant.
  Bacterial populations can develop resistance, so they work best as part of a
  combined programme rather than alone.

## Anthracnose (*Colletotrichum* spp.)

**What it looks like.** Sunken, circular lesions on ripening fruit, often with
concentric rings of small black dots (the spore masses). It is mainly a fruit
disease and is worst in wet weather.

**Management.** Use clean seed, rotate, avoid overhead irrigation, and remove
infected fruit from the field rather than dropping them. Handle fruit carefully
at harvest — wounds are the main entry point.

## Powdery Mildew (*Leveillula taurica*)

**What it looks like.** Unlike most powdery mildews, this one appears as white
powdery patches on the *underside* of the leaf, with yellowing on the upper
surface. It is common in warm, dry days with cool nights.

**Management.** Improve air movement, avoid excessive nitrogen, and remove
severely affected leaves. Neem-based sprays and sulphur are both used.

## Damping Off in the Nursery (*Pythium*, *Rhizoctonia*)

**What it looks like.** Seedlings collapse at the soil line, often in patches.
The stem is thin, watery and necrotic where it meets the soil.

**Management.** This is a soil and nursery hygiene problem. Use clean, well-drained
potting mix, do not overwater, sow thinly, and treat the nursery bed with
*Trichoderma viride* at 10-15 g per kg of potting mix. Good airflow through the
nursery is as important as any treatment.

## General prevention for pepper

1. **Seed hygiene first.** Hot water treatment or certified seed — bacterial spot
   is a seed problem before it is a field problem.
2. **Keep the canopy dry.** Drip irrigation at the base rather than sprinklers.
3. **Rotate** with a non-solanaceous crop.
4. **Clear debris** at the end of every season; do not plough infected residue
   back in.
5. **Handle plants and fruit gently.** Many pepper pathogens enter through
   wounds.
6. **Scout after rain.** Bacterial and fungal fruit rots both follow wet spells.

## Harvesting and post-harvest

Pick chilli at the right maturity for your market — green for the fresh market,
fully red for drying. For dried chilli, sun-dry on clean sheets rather than on
bare ground, and dry until the stem snaps rather than bends. Inadequate drying is
the main cause of mould and aflatoxin problems in stored chilli.

Store in clean, dry, ventilated containers, and never mix fruit from an affected
patch with sound fruit.

## A note on confidence

A photograph can suggest a disease but cannot confirm it. Nutrient deficiency,
mite damage and herbicide drift can all mimic the spotting patterns seen in
bacterial and fungal disease. Where the diagnosis matters, confirm with your local
KVK or extension officer.
