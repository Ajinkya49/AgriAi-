# Integrated Pest and Disease Management — Principles for Farmers

Source: ICAR National Research Institute for Integrated Pest Management (NRIiPM);
CEEW sustainable agriculture guidance. Compiled for Agri AI's farming assistant.

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) is not a product or a spray schedule. It is a
way of deciding *whether* to act, and it puts the cheapest and safest options
first. The order matters.

## The core idea

Treat the field as a system rather than a patient. Most pest and disease
outbreaks are the result of something that made the crop vulnerable — the wrong
variety, poor airflow, water on the leaves, unbalanced nutrition, or a field that
has grown the same crop for years. Fixing that is usually cheaper and more
lasting than spraying.

## The four levels of control, in order of preference

### 1. Cultural control — farm operations that prevent the problem

These are ordinary farm operations, not purchases, and they are the foundation.

- **Crop rotation.** Growing a different crop family in the next season breaks
  the life cycle of pathogens and pests that survive in the soil or in crop
  debris. Two seasons away from a host crop is far more effective than any
  spray.
- **Sanitation.** Remove and destroy infected plant material. Do not leave it at
  the field edge and do not compost diseased material for use on the same crop.
- **Correct spacing and pruning.** Air movement dries the leaf surface. Most
  foliar diseases need wet leaves to infect.
- **Water management.** Drip or furrow irrigation at the base, rather than
  overhead sprinklers, keeps the canopy dry and removes the main route by which
  soil-borne pathogens reach the leaves.
- **Variety choice.** A resistant variety is the most reliable single control you
  have, and it costs nothing extra at sowing time.
- **Timely sowing.** Sowing at the recommended time avoids the pest peak that a
  late or early crop runs into.

### 2. Mechanical and physical control

- Hand-picking and destroying egg masses, larvae and infected leaves.
- Traps — light traps, yellow and blue sticky traps, pheromone traps for
  monitoring and for mass trapping.
- Barriers such as nets for nurseries, and mulches that block soil splash.
- Hand weeding, since weeds harbour both pests and pathogens.

### 3. Biological control

Living organisms that suppress pests and disease. These work preventively and
need to be applied before the problem is severe.

- ***Trichoderma viride* / *T. harzianum*** — soil fungi that colonise roots,
  compete with pathogens and break down their cell walls. Used as seed treatment,
  nursery mix or soil drench.
- ***Pseudomonas fluorescens*** — a root bacterium that suppresses several foliar
  and soil-borne pathogens and is widely used in Indian vegetable production.
- ***Bacillus subtilis*** and other *Bacillus* species — used against both
  fungal and bacterial problems.
- **Conserving natural enemies.** Ladybird beetles, spiders, dragonflies and
  parasitic wasps reduce pest populations. Broad-spectrum chemical sprays kill
  them along with the pest, which is why they often make the next outbreak worse.

### 4. Chemical control — the last resort, used precisely

When the first three levels have not held, chemical control has a place, but it
must be targeted.

- **Identify the pest or disease correctly first.** Spraying for the wrong
  organism wastes money and harms natural enemies.
- **Use the recommended product at the recommended dose.** Higher doses do not
  work better and they build resistance faster.
- **Rotate chemical groups** so resistance does not develop.
- **Respect the pre-harvest interval** — the waiting period before harvest. This
  is a food safety requirement, not a suggestion.
- **Spray in the evening or early morning** to protect pollinators.

## Scouting: the habit that makes IPM work

Walk the field at least twice a week, on a fixed route. Check the oldest, lowest
leaves first, and look at the underside as well as the top. Record what you see.
The point is to catch a problem while it is still small enough that a cultural
measure or a biocontrol spray will hold it.

An economic threshold is the level of infestation at which the loss from the pest
finally exceeds the cost of treating it. Below that level, spraying loses money.
This is why "spray something just in case" is a bad habit.

## Why this matters beyond yield

Overuse of chemical pesticides in vegetables has led to resistance in several
major pests, residues above permissible limits in produce, and harm to farm
workers. Integrating cultural, mechanical and biological measures — with
chemicals used precisely and rarely — produces comparable yields with far lower
input cost and far lower risk.

## A note on confidence

Advice about pest management is only as good as the identification it rests on.
Where the diagnosis matters, confirm with your local KVK or extension officer.
